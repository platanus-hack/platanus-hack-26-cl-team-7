# Three of four pitched claims already ship

**The blunt verdict: three of your four claims describe capabilities that shipped before your hackathon, and one of those three shipped one month ago.** Full-transaction request↔response "exploit confirmation" with response-side blocking went GA at Cloudflare on **2026-10-21** ([Cloudflare blog](https://blog.cloudflare.com/full-transaction-waf/)); machine-speed deployment of CVE containment rules is mature across Cloudflare, Fastly, AWS, F5, Imperva and Qualys; and fleet "herd immunity" via shared threat intelligence is table stakes for every SaaS WAF/CDN (Fastly NLX, Akamai Client Reputation, Imperva Community Defense, CrowdSec). The reviewer who called the prototype "just another WAF with prevention capabilities" was, on those three claims, correct — and the specific sub-claims the team is most tempted to lead with (within-single-request response blocking, IP-reputation herd immunity, auto-deployment of vendor rules) have **no surviving whitespace at all**. But three genuinely unserved research questions survive, in ascending order of difficulty and descending order of competitive crowding: **cross-request (async/polling) exploit correlation**, which no shipping WAF does; **synthesis of a novel containment rule from a single observed live-exploit transaction**, which exists only in academic work (WAPP) and is known-hard because of training-data poisoning; and **a closed-loop deny→observe→classify behavioral signature that separates an attacking AI agent from a benign automated client**, which no product and no single paper does end-to-end. The fourth claim — behavioral detection of AI-agent attackers *under deliberately denied feedback* — is the only one where the team is near a real frontier rather than re-shipping prior art, and it is also the one carrying the most load-bearing caveats (no benign baseline collected yet, a "never declares success" premise partially contradicted by a November 2026 honeypot study, and an unvalidated 3× token-burn figure). If the team wants one clean metric against a baseline for a weekend, it should be built on the closed-loop behavioral detector or on cross-request correlation — not on the response-blocking or auto-patching framing, both of which a reviewer can dismiss with a single Cloudflare link.

## Claim 1: Cloudflare shipped response-side exploit confirmation on 2026-10-21 — cut the generic version, keep cross-request correlation

**What already ships.** Response-side inspection is not new on any axis. The oldest form — response-body data-leakage and PII masking — has been in **F5 BIG-IP ASM "Data Guard"** since the v11.x era ([F5 techdocs](https://techdocs.f5.com/kb/en-us/products/big-ip_asm/manuals/product/asm-implementations-11-5-0/9.html)), in **Imperva Sensitive Information Leakage Protection** ([Imperva](https://www.imperva.com/blog/the-hidden-threat-how-sensitive-information-leakage-puts-your-business-at-risk/)), in **Oracle WAF** and **Radware KWAAP** DLP modules ([Oracle](https://docs.oracle.com/en-us/iaas/Content/WAF/Reference/protectionruleids.htm) / [Radware](https://support.radware.com/app/answers/answer_view/a_id/1028812/~/radware-kwaap-data-leakage-protection)), and in the **OWASP CRS RESPONSE-950…956** rule family for ModSecurity/Coraza ([Netnea](https://www.netnea.com/cms/apache-tutorial-7_including-modsecurity-core-rules/)). A second form — response-aware login-outcome tracking — has been in **AWS WAF Fraud Control (ATP/ACFP)** since **February 2023**, inspecting CloudFront origin responses to count login success vs. failure per IP ([AWS what's new](https://aws.amazon.com/about-aws/whats-new/2023/02/aws-waf-fraud-control-account-takeover-prevention-cloudfront-origin-responses/)). The genuinely new packaging arrived **2026-10-21**, when **Cloudflare GA'd "full transaction detection"** — evaluating "each HTTP request alongside its response to identify evidence of exploitation" — plus a **"response firewall"** that blocks the origin reply before it reaches the attacker, with a worked SQLi example (suspicious request + a 200 OK whose body matches a sensitive-data signature = "Successful Exploit Confirmation") ([Cloudflare blog](https://blog.cloudflare.com/full-transaction-waf/)). This is the exact phrase — "exploit confirmation" — the team pitched, shipped to **all plans including Free** one month before the event.

**Where the state of the art stops.** Three hard limits in Cloudflare's own docs define the remaining seam. First, response custom rules **"do not support matching on `http.response.body.raw`"** — the blog states outright that "creating rules on the response body is not yet available" ([Cloudflare docs: Response firewall](https://developers.cloudflare.com/waf/response-firewall/)). Second, it correlates **only within a single transaction**; nothing in the blog or docs describes correlating request suspicion with response evidence that surfaces in a *later* request. Third, it is **edge/SaaS-only and not self-hostable**, and blocking a response "cannot undo actions already performed at the origin." The self-hosted open-source stack can match raw response bodies (CRS RESPONSE-95x) but ships it **off by default** — `SecResponseBodyAccess` is Off in both ModSecurity and Coraza, and the Coraza/Envoy WASM plugin additionally needs FULL mode, so the leak rules are "effectively inert unless an operator explicitly turns it On" ([ModSecurity Reference Manual](https://github.com/owasp-modsecurity/ModSecurity/wiki/Reference-Manual-(v3.x)); [coraza-rs issue #127](https://github.com/networking-incubator/coraza-rs/issues/127)) — and it is defeated by a trivial byte-range request ([SicuraNext](https://blog.sicuranext.com/response-filter-denial-of-service-a-new-way-to-shutdown-a-website/)). Critically, **none** of these — Cloudflare included — performs the cross-request pattern: Contrast's "Confirmed WAF alert (ADR+WAF)" is still single-request correlation across two sensors in a 5-minute window ([Contrast](https://docs.contrastsecurity.com/en/5-5-confirmed-waf-alert--adr---waf-.html)), and AWS ATP/ACFP is the only multi-request response-aware mechanism found but is scoped exclusively to login/account-creation outcomes.

**Verdict — mostly cut, one gap survives.** *Within-single-request response blocking has no whitespace* — it is a one-month-old free Cloudflare feature and a decade-old DLP feature; leading with it invites exactly the "just another WAF" dismissal, and the reviewer can close the argument with one link. What survives is **cross-request / async (polling-based) exploit correlation**: an exploit that plants or triggers in request A and whose evidence surfaces in the response to request B, or via an out-of-band poll. No mainstream WAF found does this, and the gap widens precisely in the agent/tool-call domain the team targets, where exploits complete across a correlated sequence rather than one transaction. A self-hosted build that also does **raw response-body matching safe-by-default** occupies the real seam between CRS (body matching, off by default, no correlation) and Cloudflare (correlation + managed signatures, no raw-body matching, edge-only). The honest caveat: "absence of evidence" is doing work here — no accessible analyst note confirmed that *nobody* ships cross-request correlation, only that none surfaced in primary vendor docs.

## Claim 2: machine-speed deployment is mature; machine-speed rule *invention* is not

**What already ships.** The distinction that decides this claim is **deployment vs. authoring**. Automatic *deployment* of a containment rule runs at genuine machine speed today, across both SaaS and scanner-driven models. **Cloudflare** pushes emergency managed-rule releases outside its normal seven-day cycle, and opted-in sites pick them up "automatically once the ruleset syncs, no customer action required" — for php2shell, seven Block rules went live **2026-10-21 at ~03:02 UTC** ([Cloudflare changelog](https://developers.cloudflare.com/waf/change-log/changelog/)). **Fastly** offers an "Automatic CVE Virtual Patching" toggle applying all future patches as soon as released ([Fastly docs](https://www.fastly.com/documentation/guides/next-gen-waf/virtual-patches-for-cves/)). The **scanner-to-WAF** model is faster still for known vulns: **Imperva SecureSphere** "automatically converts the vulnerability information to protection rules" from a Qualys/Rapid7/Acunetix scan ([Imperva/Rapid7 brief](https://www.imperva.com/docs/SB_Imperva_Rapid7.pdf)); **Qualys** auto-generates and deploys a WAF rule from a WAS finding to the whole cluster ([Qualys KB](https://success.qualys.com/support/s/article/000006325)); and **F5** launched (2026-09-01) automated virtual patching from Distributed Cloud Web App Scanning that turns scanner findings into enforced protection "in minutes rather than weeks" ([F5 release](https://investors.f5.com/news/news-details/2026/F5-Accelerates-Virtual-Patching-to-Manage-Emerging-Cyber-Risks-With-AI-Powered-WAF-and-Runtime-Security/default.aspx)). The window this closes is real and well-sourced: Mandiant's mean time-to-exploit has gone **negative — 5 days (2023) → −1 (2024) → −7 (2025)** ([SC World](https://www.scworld.com/news/threat-actors-exploit-zero-days-within-5-days-says-googles-mandiant)) — and ~**19–23% of CISA KEV entries are exploited on or before the CVE publication date** ([VulnCheck via stingrai](https://www.stingrai.io/blog/vulnerability-statistics-2026); [HackTheHammer](https://www.hackthehammer.com/known-exploited-vulnerabilities-statistics/)), against a reality where organizations fully remediated only **26% of detected KEV vulnerabilities**.

**Where the state of the art stops.** In every shipping product, the "automatic" refers to the *delivery pipeline* — the rule itself is authored by a vendor researcher (Cloudflare/Fastly/AWS) or fingerprinted from a *known* vulnerability by a scanner (Imperva/Qualys/F5). None claims "we observed a novel attack and synthesized a new rule." The commercial adaptive-ML systems that come closest — **Radware Auto Policy Generation** ([Radware](https://www.radware.com/cyberpedia/application-security/ai-driven-wafs-for-large-scale-deployments/)), **Fortinet FortiADC Adaptive Learning** ([Fortinet](https://docs.fortinet.com/document/fortiadc/7.6.0/new-features/530168/waf-adaptive-learning)), **Wallarm** — learn a model of *normal* traffic and output policy *recommendations*; they are not triggered by a single CVE PoC to mint a CVE-scoped signature. The only work that genuinely does "live traffic → synthesized enforceable rule" is academic: **WAPP** ("Safe Learning of Positive Security WAF Policies from Live Traffic," 2026), which does deterministic rule synthesis with confidence scoring and validation — and which exists *because* the problem is hard, explicitly addressing that "learning directly from live traffic can be unsafe when malicious requests contaminate the training data" ([arXiv 2609.06840](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.06840)). Even the "autonomous SOC" wave (CrowdStrike Charlotte AI, Fal.Con 2026-09-02) keeps containment behind a per-workflow autonomy slider defaulting to human approval, and **Gartner placed "AI SOC Agents" at the Peak of Inflated Expectations** in June 2026 ([Stellar Cyber](https://stellarcyber.ai/learn/top-10-agentic-soc-platforms/)).

**Verdict — cut the patch-gap framing, keep novel-rule synthesis as the hard gap.** The generic "machine speed vs. office hours" pitch is weak: SaaS vendors already shrink the *protection* window to hours automatically, so the quantified CVE-exploit window (real as it is) is partly already addressed. What survives is narrow and genuinely unshipped: **synthesis of a novel, exploit-specific containment rule from a single observed live-exploit transaction, auto-deployed sub-hour with no vendor or human in the loop.** No surveyed commercial product does this; only WAPP attempts it. The load-bearing caveat is the reason vendors don't ship it — **training-data poisoning**, where an attacker teaches the system a bad rule — so a prototype's credibility hinges entirely on how it prevents that, and a reviewer will ask. Note also the RASP/ADR rebuttal: runtime self-protection already "blocks exploit attempts even before a patch exists, because it monitors behavior rather than signatures" ([daily.dev](https://daily.dev/blog/what-is-rasp-runtime-application-self-protection-explained/)), so "contain at the last step" alone invites a "that's RASP" dismissal paralleling "that's a WAF." The novelty must live in the *synthesis* layer, not the containment position.

## Claim 3: herd immunity for IP reputation is table stakes; behavioral-signature sharing is thin but patent-encumbered

**What already ships.** The "one bite inoculates the fleet" value proposition is sold, by name, by multiple vendors. **Fastly's Network Learning Exchange (NLX)** shares confirmed-malicious IPs across "tens of thousands of customers' distributed software agents," and reports that **69% of IPs it tags target multiple customers, 64% multiple industries** ([Fastly product brief](https://www.fastly.com/resources/datasheets/fastly-next-gen-waf-product-brief)). **Akamai Client Reputation** correlates client requests across customers to compute per-customer risk scores ([product brief](https://atoll.gr/2015/pdf/kona-client-reputation-product-brief.pdf)), and **Akamai Threat Intelligence Service (ATIS), November 2026**, is a literal herd model: "when a new attack hits one enrolled customer, the ATIS team captures the signature and updates the rule for all of them" ([Akamai blog](https://www.akamai.com/blog/security/2026/nov/akamai-threat-intelligence-service-attack-data-action)). **Imperva ThreatRadar Community Defense** markets itself as "the first crowd-sourced threat intelligence service that aggregates and validates attack data from WAFs" ([Imperva](https://www.imperva.com/company/press_releases/imperva-introduces-crowdsourced-threat-intelligence-solution-to-secure-access-to-data-stored-in-cloud-apps/)). **Cloudflare** distributes a new detection "to our entire network… in under 30 seconds" ([Cloudflare](https://blog.cloudflare.com/frontier-model-defense/)). **AWS** ships `AWSManagedRulesAmazonIpReputationList`, auto-updated from its MadPot honeypot network. On the open-source side, **CrowdSec** shares an offending IP plus the *scenario* (behavior) that triggered it, curated and redistributed, with community blocklists "updated several times a day" ([CrowdSec FAQ](https://www.crowdsec.net/faq)), and single-org minute-scale sync of decision lists is a solved problem via CrowdSec LAPI + `cs-blocklist-mirror` ([CrowdSec docs](https://docs.crowdsec.net/docs/configuration/crowdsec_configuration/)). Cross-org IoC sharing is served by **MISP** (push/pull/cherry-pick, used by NATO and CERTs) ([MISP](https://www.misp-project.org/features/)).

**Where the state of the art stops.** The fast, automatic propagation is almost always **IP reputation** — a list. Behavioral/attack-pattern propagation (NLX "attack patterns," Akamai ATIS signatures, Imperva "attack patterns," MISP TTPs) is slower and **analyst-curated**, not an automatic site-to-site reflex. And IP reputation is structurally too late: GreyNoise found **78% of edge-targeting IPs vanish before any reputation system can flag them**, and 39% come from residential connections where rotation outruns any feed ([GreyNoise](https://www.greynoise.io/resources/invisible-army-residential-proxy-abuse-report)); independently, **45% of behaviorally-hostile actors had AbuseIPDB scores below 50** ([dev.to](https://dev.to/botconductstandard/45-of-hostile-bot-traffic-passes-your-waf-heres-why-what-behavioral-detection-reveals-when-you-12mh)). MISP, the cross-org standard, is "fundamentally passive and retrospective… designed for post-incident forensic dissemination rather than real-time, reactive containment" ([arXiv 2605.17325](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.17325)).

**Verdict — cut IP-reputation herd immunity entirely; the surviving gap is thin and patent-shadowed.** *IP-reputation herd immunity has no whitespace* — it is table stakes (Fastly, Akamai, Imperva, CrowdSec, AWS all ship it) and is undermined by its own staleness. The only survivor is **automatic, real-time, fleet-wide propagation of request-shape / attack-progression signatures with per-instance confidence weighting, low enough FP risk to auto-enforce** — the thing MISP's passivity and CrowdSec's IP-centricity leave open. Three load-bearing caveats make this the weakest of the surviving gaps. First, **patents already claim the concept**: a WAF analytics server where "generated rules… are shared among all WAFs" ([US 11,991,149](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11991149)), a cooperative IDS exchanging reputation "in real-time, or near real-time" ([US 8,925,082](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8925082)), and an automatic signature-propagation network ([US 8,510,834](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8510834)) — so "novel" needs care. Second, shared signals **inherit the originator's false-positive rate**: CrowdSec's own 2026 incident blocked Facebook's crawler and Censys scanners via community blocklist ([GitHub #4518](https://github.com/crowdsecurity/crowdsec/issues/4518)). Third, **poisoning** systematically degrades shared detectors — an LLM-adversarial study on CTI showed false negatives rising 734→5,537 and false positives 638→2,373 over retraining rounds ([arXiv 2507.06252](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.06252v2)). This gap is real but it is the hardest to defend as novel and the easiest to get wrong.

## Claim 4: closed-loop deny→observe→classify is the one real frontier — and the one with the shakiest premises

**What already ships (and why it's adjacent, not identical).** Every component of this claim exists *somewhere*, which is why it reads as prior art at first glance. The **confirm→redirect→feed-fabricated-responses** pipeline is decades-old patented deception, described explicitly in production patents where a deception module redirects the attacker and supplies "misinformation that his attack is successful" ([US 11831420](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11831420)). **Tarpits** — Cloudflare **AI Labyrinth** (March 2025), Nepenthes, Iocaine, Quixotic — already deny the agent useful output and burn its compute, and Labyrinth even "doubles as a sensor" by fingerprinting maze-walkers ([primores.org](https://primores.org/blog/ai-crawler-tarpits/)). **Bot-and-agent-management leaders** have pivoted hard to AI-agent intent: the **Forrester Wave Q2 2026** is now titled "Bot *and Agent* Trust Management" ([Prosopo](https://prosopo.io/blog/forrester-wave-bot-management-2026/)), **DataDome's Agent Trust** engine (2025) evaluates "behavioral sequencing, endpoint sensitivity, request rate, session context" in <2 ms ([Help Net Security](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2025/04/15/datadome-ai-engine/)), and DataDome found **80% of AI agents don't properly identify themselves**, forcing the move to behavioral signals ([DataDome](https://datadome.co/threat-research/state-of-bot-and-agent-security-report-key-ai-findings/)). Defender-side behavioral tells are already published: **Resecurity** names "self-correcting retry patterns (same endpoint, slightly different parameter each time)" and no-human-think-time sequencing ([Resecurity](https://www.resecurity.com/blog/article/when-ai-becomes-the-attacker-understanding-autonomous-offensive-security-agents)), and the offensive agent **XBOW** itself "mutated the string to bypass the WAF" ([CISO Series](https://cisoseries.com/automating-offensive-security-with-xbow/)) — real-world confirmation of the first half of the team's signature, from the attacker vendor's own mouth.

**Where the state of the art stops.** Critically, every one of these is either **open-loop** or **passive**. Tarpits deny output but never read the reaction back as a classifier. Bot managers *observe* sequences or issue challenges; none describes *actively denying or falsifying feedback and measuring the reaction*. Classic deception optimizes for attacker *belief* (keep them engaged on fake assets) — the exact opposite of the team's goal of provoking a measurable *malfunction*. The closest prior art is a cluster of **late-2026 academic prototypes**, and each stops short of the team's exact mechanism. **"A Gingerbread House for LLM Agents" (arXiv 2611.07611, Nov 2026)** confirms an attacker, routes every subsequent request to a shadow service, measures false-success reporting ("reported a planted fake secret as genuine on 30 of 120 tasks"), and *incidentally* observed thrashing ("31,409 requests to an upload path that does not exist") — but it feeds **false success, not denial**, and explicitly does not instrument token burn, payload mutation, or give-up-vs-persist ([arXiv 2611.07611](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.07611v1)). **The narrative-coherence honeypot study (arXiv 2611.22589, Nov 2026)** directly measures persistence — raising median session length "from 40 to 165 commands" and cutting abandonment "from 86% to 52%" ([arXiv 2611.22589](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.22589)). **CHeaT (USENIX Security 2025)** shows the "follows an obvious trap" tell ([USENIX PDF](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/usenixsecurity25-ayzenshteyn.pdf)). Token amplification exists but in a DoS regime — "From Shield to Target" shows **13–63× token amplification** under adversarial input ([arXiv 2606.14517](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.14517)).

**Verdict — keep this; it is the only claim near a real frontier.** The team's four-part signature (≈3× token burn, payload mutation, never declares success, never gives up) **decomposes into existing findings**, but the *integration* — using **feedback denial as the deliberate stimulus** and the **composite thrash signature as a real-time classifier that separates attacker-agent from benign client** — is built by no found product and no single paper. Of all prior art, only Gingerbread closes a loop, and it reads false-reporting, not thrash dynamics. This is the defensible wedge *and* the one weekend-scale experiment with a clean single metric (classification separation under denial vs. a benign baseline). But three caveats are load-bearing and the researchers flagged each. **(1) No benign-client baseline exists yet** — no found work contrasts attacker thrash metrics against legitimate automated clients on identical signals; this is simultaneously the whitespace and the mandatory validation the team has not done. **(2) "Never declares success" is partially contradicted** by the narrative-coherence study's finding that a free-running LLM "tends to declare itself satisfied at arbitrary points," so the claim must be narrowed to *"never declares success specifically under sustained feedback denial."* **(3) The ~3× token-burn multiplier is uncorroborated** for the confirm-then-deny setup — the external figure (13–63×) is from a different DoS regime, so 3× must be presented as the team's own prototype measurement, not a validated constant.

## The ranked survivors: three research questions, not four product features

Stripped of product framing, four candidate gaps remain, and they sort cleanly by how much defensible whitespace each actually has. **No whitespace (cut on sight):** within-single-request response blocking (Cloudflare Free, 2026-10-21); IP-reputation herd immunity (Fastly/Akamai/Imperva/CrowdSec/AWS); auto-deployment of vendor- or scanner-authored patches (mature across six vendors); and "we detect AI agents behaviorally" (DataDome/HUMAN/Kasada own this). Any of these as the headline guarantees the "just another WAF" verdict. The three questions worth a weekend, research-question-first:

| Rank | Research question (not a feature) | Who comes closest | Why the gap survives | Hardest caveat |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | *Does denying success feedback to a confirmed attacking agent produce a composite behavioral signature (token burn, payload mutation, persistence) that a benign automated client provably does not show?* | Gingerbread House (feeds false success, not denial; no thrash instrumentation) | Closed-loop deny→observe→classify is built by no product and no single paper | No benign baseline collected; "never declares success" needs narrowing; 3× unvalidated |
| 2 | *Can a proxy correlate request-side suspicion with response evidence that surfaces in a later, separate request (async/polling exploit)?* | Cloudflare full-transaction (single transaction only); AWS ATP (auth-only) | No mainstream WAF does cross-request correlation; gap widens for agentic tool-call sequences | Rests on absence-of-evidence; no analyst note accessible to confirm |
| 3 | *Can a system synthesize a novel containment rule from one observed live-exploit transaction and auto-deploy it sub-hour with no human in the loop?* | WAPP (academic only); no commercial product | Commercial "automatic" = deploying a human-authored rule; invention is still human | Training-data poisoning is the unsolved reason vendors don't ship it |
| 4 (weak) | *Can request-shape/attack-progression signatures propagate fleet-wide in real time, auto-enforced, without analyst curation?* | Akamai ATIS, Fastly NLX (both analyst-curated for behavior) | Behavioral propagation is thin and slow across the fleet | Patents already claim rule propagation; inherited-FP and poisoning both unsolved |

The single clean metric that fits a weekend and is hardest to dismiss is #1's: **classification separation (AUROC or TPR at a fixed benign false-alarm rate) between an attacking agent and a benign automated client, measured under deliberately denied feedback, against a passive-observation baseline.** That is the one framing where the answer to "why bother, doesn't Cloudflare do this?" is a defensible *no* — because Cloudflare, every bot manager, and every tarpit are open-loop or passive, and the loop is the contribution.

## Conclusion

The honest reading is that the hackathon team pitched a four-capability product where three capabilities are, respectively, a one-month-old free feature, a mature six-vendor category, and a table-stakes network effect — and the reviewer's "nothing complex, ambitious, or exciting" landed because the pitch led with the product surface (a WAF that blocks responses and shares intel) rather than the one research question underneath it that nobody has answered. The strategic move is not to defend the WAF; it is to **discard the WAF framing entirely and keep the one closed-loop measurement** — whether denied feedback elicits a separable behavioral signature from an attacking agent versus a benign client — because that is the only claim that survives contact with the prior art, and it survives precisely because the entire deception/tarpit/bot-management industry is open-loop by design. This also aligns with the prior pivot assessment's conclusion to "keep the evidence infrastructure, cut the WAF feature roadmap" ([pivot memo](./AI%20safety%20hackathon%20pivot%20assessment.md)): the reusable asset is the instrumented confirm-then-deny harness and the ability to record what an agent does when it cannot tell whether it succeeded, not the blocking. The uncomfortable implication the team should internalize before presenting: the closed-loop detector's novelty is *entirely* contingent on producing the benign-client baseline it does not yet have — without it, the result is indistinguishable from the published observational tells (Resecurity, CHeaT, Gingerbread) and collapses back into "just another behavioral bot detector." The weekend's real deliverable is therefore not the detector but the **baseline contrast** — the one experiment the frontier has not run.
