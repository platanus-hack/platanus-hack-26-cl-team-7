# Fleet / Network-Effect Shared Threat Intelligence — WAF Prior-Art Due Diligence

Scope note: Current as of November 2026. Throughout, I distinguish **IP-reputation sharing** (who attacked) from **behavioral / exploit-pattern sharing** (how they attacked — request shape, attacker playbook). The headline for the hackathon team: fleet-level "inoculation" is real and fast for *IP reputation and vendor-authored signatures*, but near-real-time sharing of *behavioral/attack-progression signatures across an arbitrary fleet* is thin, mostly vendor-internal, and largely absent for self-hosted/cross-org deployments.

## Q1: How do Cloudflare, AWS WAF, Akamai, Imperva, Fastly, and bot vendors propagate a newly seen attack pattern to all customers? How fast, automatic or curated?

### Takeaway
All major SaaS WAF/CDN networks have a genuine "herd" effect, but it operates at two different speeds and levels: (a) **vendor-authored managed rules/ML models** push network-wide fast (Cloudflare claims <30s distribution once deployed; hours from PoC to rule), and (b) **crowd-sourced IP reputation feeds** (Fastly NLX, Akamai Client Reputation, Imperva Community Defense) propagate "near-real-time." Propagation of the *rule content* is automatic; authorship of new behavioral signatures is human/analyst-curated, not an automatic site-to-site reflex.

### Cited Findings
- **Cloudflare — distribution speed:** "Once a detection is deployed, it reaches our entire network, along with every Cloudflare customer, in under 30 seconds." — [Cloudflare blog: Frontier model defense](https://blog.cloudflare.com/frontier-model-defense/)
- **Cloudflare — authoring speed:** Traditional SLA "from a fresh proof-of-concept to a live, deployed rule has been 12 hours"; team now releases rules "within hours of a proof-of-concept." React2Shell cited as a managed rule live hours before the official advisory. — [Cloudflare: Frontier model defense](https://blog.cloudflare.com/frontier-model-defense/)
- **Cloudflare — what is shared network-wide:** managed WAF rulesets/signatures available to every customer; ML "WAF Attack Score" (1–99 based on how closely a request resembles known attack *shapes*, not known-bad signatures); Cloudforce One IOCs usable inside WAF; Bot Management scores "using the same signals across our whole network." — [Cloudflare: Frontier model defense](https://blog.cloudflare.com/frontier-model-defense/)
- **Cloudflare — ML trained on fleet:** "By observing traffic across millions of sites," the network builds baselines of legitimate fingerprints; a detection improvement "is already ingesting data from the entire Cloudflare network, automatically catching more malicious traffic for all customers." All customers "benefit automatically from ongoing improvements through machine learning detection." — [Cloudflare: Per-customer bot defenses](https://blog.cloudflare.com/per-customer-bot-defenses/)
- **Cloudflare — curation layer:** Threat Signals "automatically parses open-source threat reporting, extracts structured indicators, and connects threat context directly to WAF rules." — [Cloudflare: Threat Signals](https://blog.cloudflare.com/threat-signals/)
- **AWS WAF — IP reputation managed group:** `AWSManagedRulesAmazonIpReputationList` is "updated frequently based on the evolution of Amazon threat intelligence"; **no versioning, no fixed cadence, and no SNS/update notifications** for this group. IP list sourced partly from MadPot (Amazon's threat-intel honeypot network). Updates are automatic. — [AWS docs: IP reputation rule groups](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/waf/latest/developerguide/aws-managed-rule-groups-ip-rep.html); [AWS Managed Rules best practices](https://aws.github.io/aws-security-services-best-practices/guides/waf/aws-managed-rules/docs/)
- **AWS — note:** Other AWS managed rule groups (non-IP-rep) *do* use versioning; the default version typically updates ~7 days after a new release. So AWS's fast/silent path is specifically the IP-reputation feed, not behavioral rules. — [AWS Managed Rules best practices](https://aws.github.io/aws-security-services-best-practices/guides/waf/aws-managed-rules/docs/)
- **Akamai Client Reputation — cross-customer correlation:** "correlation of client requests across different customers to identify malicious intent"; unlike single-score IP reputation, it computes a **per-customer** risk score per source IP, using attacker persistency, number of targeted applications, severity, magnitude, industry, previous attacks. Built on Cloud Security Intelligence (Akamai sees 15–30% of web traffic, 1.3B devices/day). — [Akamai Client Reputation product brief (PDF)](https://atoll.gr/2015/pdf/kona-client-reputation-product-brief.pdf); [Darkreading: Cloud Security Intelligence](https://www.darkreading.com/cloud-security/akamai-cloud-security-intelligence-provides-foundation-for-security-services)
- **Akamai Threat Intelligence Service (ATIS), Nov 2026 — explicit herd model:** a group of enrolled customers share a "managed custom rule maintained by Akamai analysts. When a new attack hits one enrolled customer, the ATIS team captures the signature and updates the rule for all of them"; each customer chooses alert vs. enforce; analysts work to keep FPs down. Premise: "threat actors reuse their infrastructure, tooling, and egress paths across targets." This is **analyst-mediated (curated), not automatic.** — [Akamai: Threat Intelligence Service turns attack data into action](https://www.akamai.com/blog/security/2026/nov/akamai-threat-intelligence-service-attack-data-action)
- **Akamai — adaptive engine efficacy:** customers using adaptive intelligence reduced missed attacks "to a negligible 0.02%." — [Akamai: Threat Intelligence Service](https://www.akamai.com/blog/security/2026/nov/akamai-threat-intelligence-service-attack-data-action)
- **Imperva ThreatRadar Community Defense:** marketed as "the first crowd-sourced threat intelligence service that aggregates and validates attack data from WAFs." Gathers live attack data from WAFs worldwide, translates it into "attack patterns, reputation data, and policies," and distributes "in near-real time to fortify the entire community." (Later rebranded Client Reputation Services Community Defense.) Strictly single-vendor/closed. — [Imperva press release](https://www.imperva.com/company/press_releases/imperva-introduces-crowdsourced-threat-intelligence-solution-to-secure-access-to-data-stored-in-cloud-apps/); [ThreatRadar datasheet (PDF)](https://www.imperva.com/docs/DS_ThreatRadar_Community_Edition_and_ThreatRadar_Enterprise_Edition.pdf)
- **Fastly Next-Gen WAF — Network Learning Exchange (NLX):** "a collective threat feed... identifies and shares potentially threatening IP addresses across customer networks," based on "anonymized, confirmed malicious activity collected from tens of thousands of customers' distributed software agents." The Cloud Engine aggregates attacks across all agents (including other customers'); "when enough malicious activity is seen," the IP is processed through NLX. Customers control what data is shared. — [Fastly Next-Gen WAF product brief](https://www.fastly.com/resources/datasheets/fastly-next-gen-waf-product-brief); [Signal Sciences / Fastly NGWAF](https://appsecsanta.com/signal-sciences)
- **Fastly NLX — cross-customer reuse stat:** "69% of IPs tagged by NLX targeted multiple customers, and 64% targeted multiple industries." — [Fastly Next-Gen WAF product brief](https://www.fastly.com/resources/datasheets/fastly-next-gen-waf-product-brief)

### Inferences
- The industry already sells exactly the "one bite inoculates the fleet" value prop — Fastly NLX, Akamai ATIS, and Imperva Community Defense are literal implementations. The hackathon's "just another WAF" dismissal is partly because this network effect is table stakes for SaaS WAFs.
- The crucial seam: the *fast, automatic* propagation is almost always **IP reputation** (a list), while **behavioral signature** propagation (NLX "attack patterns," Akamai ATIS) is slower and analyst-curated. The automatic reflex "one site attacked → every site's behavioral rules updated in minutes without a human" is NOT the default state of the art.

### Gaps
- No primary source gives a precise end-to-end latency for NLX or Imperva Community Defense ("near-real-time" is unquantified). Cloudflare's <30s is distribution-only, excluding authoring time.

## Q2: Crowd-sourced / collaborative networks (CrowdSec, Project Honey Pot, GreyNoise, AbuseIPDB, Spamhaus) — what/how fast/quality?

### Takeaway
These share primarily **malicious-IP reputation and coarse behavior labels** (scanner/harvester/spammer), not request-shape exploit signatures. Propagation is minutes-to-several-times-daily. Quality is the central problem: community lists carry inherited false positives and the IPs rotate out fast. CrowdSec is the one that shares *behavioral scenario* context plus IP, and is the most relevant open-source analog to the pitch.

### Cited Findings
- **CrowdSec — what's shared:** signals combine an offending IP with the *scenario* (behavior) that triggered it; "Data collected worldwide is curated... then redistributed to each user based on their self-declared technological footprint." Curation uses "reporter trust score and diversity, and machine profiling." — [CrowdSec FAQ](https://www.crowdsec.net/faq); [CrowdSec blocklists intro](https://docs.crowdsec.net/u/blocklists/intro/)
- **CrowdSec — speed/churn:** community blocklists "updated several times a day," with "~5% daily rotation on average." An IP enters the community blocklist only after "enough reports" (confidence threshold), because "false-positives can be damaging for many users." — [CrowdSec blocklists](https://www.crowdsec.net/blocklists); [CrowdSec FAQ](https://www.crowdsec.net/faq)
- **CrowdSec — FP reality:** markets "zero false positives" on its own curated lists but cannot guarantee third-party lists are FP-free. Documented 2026 incident: Facebook's `facebookexternalhit` crawler blocked via community-blocklist (an alert showed `crowdsecurity/community-blocklist` adding +15,000 IPs via CAPI). Censys scanner IPs were only excluded after Censys contacted CrowdSec and followed a security-company whitelist process. — [GitHub issue #4518](https://github.com/crowdsecurity/crowdsec/issues/4518); [Censys FP discourse thread](https://discourse.crowdsec.net/t/http-bad-user-agent-false-positive-for-censys-scans/2213)
- **Project Honey Pot (http:BL):** tracks "harvesters, comment spammers, and other suspicious visitors." Data served over DNS; response gives visitor *type* (bitset: 1=suspicious, 2=harvester, 4=comment spammer), threat score, and **days since last seen** in the trap network. Free to active members (install a honeypot / donate an MX / add a QuickLink). Integrations: mod_httpbl, ModSecurity, vBulletin, SMF. — [Project Honey Pot http:BL overview](https://www.projecthoneypot.org/httpbl.php); [http:BL API](https://www.projecthoneypot.org/httpbl_api.php)
- **GreyNoise — what/how fast:** "thousands of sensors worldwide," "constantly updating databases in real-time"; each IP tagged with behavior, associated CVEs, and actor. Classification split into Intent (Unknown/Malicious/Benign) and Category (Activity/Tool/Actor/Worm/Search Engine). "As soon as sensors see activity, the behavior is tagged and visible to customers." — [GreyNoise: Anatomy of a GreyNoise Tag](https://www.greynoise.io/blog/anatomy-of-a-greynoise-tag); [GreyNoise IP details](https://www.greynoise.io/features/product-feature-ip-details)
- **AbuseIPDB — what/how fast:** community database where users report IPs (SSH brute force, port scan, spam, web attacks); reports roll into a "confidence of abuse" percentage; fast abuse signal, simple API, generous free tier. — [hunt.io: IP reputation tools](https://hunt.io/glossary/best-ip-reputation-enrichment-tools)
- **AbuseIPDB — quality caveat:** "coverage is uneven: a fresh attacker IP may have zero reports, and shared infrastructure can accumulate reports that don't reflect current ownership." — [hunt.io: IP reputation tools](https://hunt.io/glossary/best-ip-reputation-enrichment-tools)
- **Spamhaus:** primarily email reputation/blocklists (DNSBL) — distinct from request-shape behavioral tagging. — [hunt.io: IP reputation tools](https://hunt.io/glossary/best-ip-reputation-enrichment-tools)

### Inferences
- CrowdSec is the closest open-source prior art to "vaccinate the fleet from one bite": it already shares behavior-scenario + IP signals globally with curation. The pitch must differentiate on *what* is shared (request-shape / attack-progression signatures) vs. CrowdSec's IP-centric model.
- Project Honey Pot's "days since last seen" field is itself an admission that IP reputation is time-decaying — directly supports the staleness argument.

### Gaps
- No single authoritative latency number for AbuseIPDB report → queryable (reports surface quickly but exact SLA unstated).

## Q3: Is anything shared at the level of EXPLOIT BEHAVIOR / attack-progression signatures (not just IP reputation)?

### Takeaway
Mostly no at the cross-fleet, real-time, automatic level. Vendors share **IP reputation fast** and **behavioral/attack-pattern signatures slowly and analyst-curated** (NLX "attack patterns," Akamai ATIS signatures, Imperva "attack patterns," MISP TTPs). The literature and vendor data strongly validate that IP reputation is structurally too slow because IPs rotate faster than feeds update — which is the opening for request-shape/playbook sharing, but no major player does automatic fleet-wide behavioral-signature propagation as a reflex.

### Cited Findings
- **IP reputation is structurally late (the core argument for behavioral sharing):** "Reputation tells you what an IP did somewhere else, after someone reported it. Behavior tells you what an actor is doing on your infrastructure, right now, before anyone else sees it... By the time an IP earns a reputation, the actor has rotated to a new one." — [dev.to: 45% of hostile bot traffic passes your WAF](https://dev.to/botconductstandard/45-of-hostile-bot-traffic-passes-your-waf-heres-why-what-behavioral-detection-reveals-when-you-12mh)
- **Quantified reputation gap:** "Of 240 hostile actors detected by behavior over 19 days, operating from 380 distinct IPs, 45% have AbuseIPDB scores below 50" — i.e., hostile by behavior but not yet reputationally flagged. — [dev.to: 45% of hostile bot traffic](https://dev.to/botconductstandard/45-of-hostile-bot-traffic-passes-your-waf-heres-why-what-behavioral-detection-reveals-when-you-12mh)
- **GreyNoise on rotation outrunning feeds:** "IP reputation is structurally broken against residential proxies because the rotation rate exceeds the update cycle of any feed-based defense." GreyNoise found **39% of unique IPs targeting the edge come from home internet connections, and 78% vanish before any reputation system can flag them.** — [GreyNoise: Invisible Army residential proxy report](https://www.greynoise.io/resources/invisible-army-residential-proxy-abuse-report)
- **Behavioral sharing exists but is pattern/signature-curated, not automatic reflex:** Fastly NLX "uniquely recognizes attack patterns across the customer network"; Imperva Community Defense "translate[s]... into attack patterns, policies, and reputation data." Both are vendor-processed. — [Fastly product brief](https://www.fastly.com/resources/datasheets/fastly-next-gen-waf-product-brief); [Imperva datasheet (PDF)](https://www.imperva.com/docs/DS_ThreatRadar_Community_Edition_and_ThreatRadar_Enterprise_Edition.pdf)
- **MISP can carry behavioral/TTP intel but is passive/retrospective:** MISP models "threat actors, campaigns, vulnerabilities, tactics and techniques" via MISP galaxy and MITRE ATT&CK mappings. But an academic analysis notes platforms like MISP are "highly effective for sharing passive Indicators of Compromise... However, these sharing models are fundamentally passive and retrospective. They are designed for post-incident forensic dissemination rather than real-time, reactive containment." — [MISP features](https://www.misp-project.org/features/); [arXiv 2605.17325 (federated stream-processing)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.17325)
- **Patents show the concept is known prior art:** a WAF analytics server where "generated rules (or policies) are shared among all WAFs"; signatures can be scoped generic / generic-source / generic-target. A cooperative IDS cluster exchanges "IP reputation influencing events in real-time, or near real-time." — [US Patent 11,991,149 (analytics-based WAF config)](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11991149); [US Patent 8,925,082 (cooperative IDS ecosystem)](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8925082); [US Patent 8,510,834 (automatic signature propagation network)](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8510834)

### Inferences
- The genuinely under-served niche is **automatic, real-time sharing of attack-progression / request-shape signatures across a fleet** (not IPs, not analyst-curated patterns). The evidence (GreyNoise 78%, the 45% AbuseIPDB-miss stat) is a strong pitch for why IP-reputation herd immunity is insufficient — but note patents already claim rule/signature propagation, so "novel" needs care.
- MISP is the cross-org behavioral-sharing standard, but its passive/retrospective nature leaves a real-time automatic-containment gap the team could target.

### Gaps
- Could not find a shipping product that automatically propagates *attacker-playbook/request-shape* signatures fleet-wide in minutes without analyst curation. Absence of evidence here is itself useful signal for the pitch (but not proof none exists).

## Q4: Can a self-hosted proxy fleet sync dynamic rules in minutes with off-the-shelf tooling? Cross-org sharing?

### Takeaway
Yes for a single org's own fleet: CrowdSec (central LAPI or blocklist-mirror) and MISP give off-the-shelf minute-scale sync of IP/decision lists today. Cross-*organization* real-time sharing is served by MISP (push/pull peer-to-peer) and CrowdSec's community CAPI, but both are IP/IoC-centric and, for MISP, more batch/forensic than reactive.

### Cited Findings
- **CrowdSec single-org fleet sync (central LAPI):** bouncers query the Local API (LAPI) to "fetch active decisions and apply them in real-time"; standard multi-server setup uses one central LAPI serving all agents/bouncers. — [CrowdSec configuration docs](https://docs.crowdsec.net/docs/configuration/crowdsec_configuration/); [Deploying CrowdSec centrally](https://www.bujarra.com/desplegando-crowdsec-de-manera-centralizada/?lang=en)
- **CrowdSec blocklist mirror (feed appliances):** `cs-blocklist-mirror` exposes active decisions over HTTP (e.g. `http://localhost:41412/security/blocklist`) for firewalls/appliances; `apt install crowdsec-blocklist-mirror`, register with `cscli bouncers add`. — [github.com/crowdsecurity/cs-blocklist-mirror](https://github.com/crowdsecurity/cs-blocklist-mirror); [akmalovaa/crowdsec-blocklist mirror](https://github.com/akmalovaa/crowdsec-blocklist)
- **Self-hosted multi-node pattern without community sharing:** real-world PRAHO design runs standalone CrowdSec per node (agent → local LAPI → nftables bouncer) and a platform "sync task" that copies only self-originated bans (`origin=crowdsec`) between nodes; "no signals are sent to CrowdSec, so no community blocklist." Confirms you can build a private minute-scale sync today. — [PRAHO Issue #767](https://github.com/captainpragmatic/PRAHO/issues/767)
- **CrowdSec HA gap:** community discussion notes no built-in multi-LAPI HA / decision sync between LAPIs; workaround is "send decisions to a private blocklist shared by all bouncers." So native cross-LAPI sync is DIY. — [PRAHO Issue #767](https://github.com/captainpragmatic/PRAHO/issues/767)
- **MISP cross-org sync:** peer-to-peer architecture with three sync methods — **push, pull, cherry-pick**; distribution tiers "Your organisation only / This community only / Connected communities / All communities," plus Sharing Groups for precise audiences. Actively developed since 2011, used by Belgian Defense, NATO, CERTs, ISACs. — [MISP features](https://www.misp-project.org/features/); [Help Net Security: MISP](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2024/08/05/misp-open-source-threat-intelligence-platform/)
- **MISP limitation for real-time:** "fundamentally passive and retrospective... post-incident forensic dissemination rather than real-time, reactive containment." — [arXiv 2605.17325](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.17325)

### Inferences
- Off-the-shelf minute-scale sync of *IP/decision lists* within one org is a solved problem (CrowdSec LAPI + blocklist-mirror). The team cannot claim novelty there.
- The genuine white space for self-hosted/cross-org: automatic, near-real-time sync of **behavioral/request-shape rules** (not IP lists) with low enough FP risk to auto-enforce. MISP's passivity and CrowdSec's IP-centricity leave this open.

### Gaps
- No evidence of an off-the-shelf tool that syncs *behavioral attack-progression signatures* (vs. IPs/IoCs) across a self-hosted fleet in minutes. This is a defensible differentiation claim but rests on absence-of-evidence.

## Q5: Known weaknesses of shared threat intel (poisoning, false positives, staleness, privacy)

### Takeaway
All four weaknesses are well-documented in both vendor incidents and peer-reviewed literature. The dominant practical lesson: shared signals inherit the originator's false-positive rate, poisoning systematically degrades detectors over repeated retraining, IP IoCs go stale within hours-to-days (rotation outpaces feeds), and curation/anonymization are the main mitigations — none perfect.

### Cited Findings
- **Inherited FP / auto-block propagation risk (CrowdSec-specific analysis):** "a shared signal inherits the false-positive rate of whichever instance originally generated it... a bad signal from one participating organization can propagate as an auto-applied block decision to every other subscribed instance." Recommended mitigation: treat community signals as "one input to a confidence-weighted decision rather than an automatic remediation trigger," requiring a minimum confidence threshold or local corroboration. — [perun.au: CrowdSec in production — five failure patterns](https://perun.au/insights/crowdsec-production)
- **Poisoning degrades the FP/FN balance (empirical):** LLM-based adversarial attacks on text-based CTI produced "a systematic increase in false negatives (from 734 to 5,537) and false positives (from 638 to 2,373) over retraining rounds." Early-stage injection didn't immediately degrade; "with continued injection over multiple retraining rounds, the model exhibited a considerable drop in recall and overall F1 score, demonstrating the cumulative impact of progressive data poisoning." — [arXiv 2507.06252: False Alarms, Real Damage](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.06252v2)
- **Poisoning as structural weakness:** "such poisoning throws off the balance between false positives and false negatives thereby dramatically reducing the efficacy of the detector"; an attacker can evade by "carefully adding moderate 'poisoned data' at different intervals." — [CSIR: 'Data Poisoning' — Achilles Heel of CTI Systems (PDF)](https://researchspace.csir.co.za/bitstream/handle/10204/10853/Mahlangu_22278_2019.pdf)
- **Independent-validation recommendation:** compromised CTI artifacts "erode the effectiveness of intelligence-sharing ecosystems" and "necessitates independent validation mechanisms for CTI objects, such as provenance tracking, cryptographic integrity verification, and adversarial testing." — [Journal of Cybersecurity (Oxford), 2024/25](https://academic.oup.com/cybersecurity/article/11/1/tyaf016/8244123)
- **Low feed overlap / collateral damage (coverage + FP):** "little overlap in the different data sources"; "acquiring different CTI sources is unlikely to provide better coverage and may even cause collateral damage due to false positives." — [MDPI JCP: Sharing ML Models as IoCs](https://doi.org/10.3390/jcp1010008)
- **Staleness / rotation (quantified):** "78% [of edge-targeting IPs] vanish before any reputation system can flag them"; rotation rate "exceeds the update cycle of any feed-based defense." — [GreyNoise: Invisible Army](https://www.greynoise.io/resources/invisible-army-residential-proxy-abuse-report)
- **Staleness design acknowledgment:** http:BL returns "how long it has been since the visitor has last been seen," and academic work on "Decaying Indicators of Compromise" exists (IoCs need explicit decay/expiry). — [Project Honey Pot http:BL](https://www.projecthoneypot.org/httpbl.php); [arXiv 1803.11052: Decaying IoCs](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1803.11052)
- **Privacy / anonymization as mitigation:** Fastly NLX uses "anonymized, confirmed malicious activity" and gives "customers control over what data is shared"; threat intelligence is handled "in a manner that does not associate the threat intelligence with or identify any subscriber." CrowdSec curates/anonymizes before redistribution (GDPR stance in FAQ). — [Fastly product brief](https://www.fastly.com/resources/datasheets/fastly-next-gen-waf-product-brief); [CrowdSec FAQ](https://www.crowdsec.net/faq)
- **False-positive suppression persists (operational debt):** detection-rule repositories accumulate and persist FP-suppression (the "Exclusion Ratchet"); cites "99% false positives" SOC-analyst study (USENIX Security 2022). — [arXiv 2608.31062: The Exclusion Ratchet](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.31062)

### Inferences
- The strongest technical counter to "just another WAF": IP-reputation herd immunity is undermined by staleness (78% rotate out) and inherited FPs; a system sharing *behavioral/request-shape* signals with per-instance confidence weighting and provenance could claim to fix both — but must itself answer poisoning and the inherited-FP problem, which the literature says are unsolved in the general case.
- Every mitigation in the literature (confidence weighting, provenance, cryptographic integrity, decay/expiry, anonymization, local corroboration) is a feature checklist the pitch should explicitly address to avoid replicating known failure modes.

### Gaps
- Could not decode the "Decaying IoCs" PDF (arXiv 1803.11052) for a specific half-life/lifetime statistic; the GreyNoise 78%-vanish figure is the best quantified staleness proxy found. A precise median IP-indicator lifetime number was not located in a primary source.
